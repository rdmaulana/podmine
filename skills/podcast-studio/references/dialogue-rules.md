# Dialogue rules

The script is the product. Voice quality hides a weak script for about thirty seconds, then the listener leaves.

## Turn shape

- One to three sentences per turn. A turn over four sentences is a monologue; split it.
- Alternate. Two turns by the same host in a row only when the second is a deliberate beat — and then make it a separate short turn, not a paragraph.
- A turn should end somewhere the other host can pick up: a claim, a question, a half-objection, a number.

## The two roles

Give each host a job and keep it for the whole episode.

**Host A — the listener's stand-in.** Asks what a smart person who does not know this subject would ask. Names the obvious objection. Says "wait, so what does that mean for…". Does not pretend to know things.

**Host B — the person who has done the reading.** Answers in concrete terms, gives the number, names the tradeoff, admits what is unknown. Does not lecture; hands the floor back.

Swap the roles if the source is first-person — the person who lived it should be Host B.

## What makes it sound spoken

- Contractions. Always.
- Short sentences next to long ones. Vary the rhythm.
- One idea per sentence, not three clauses stacked.
- Concrete nouns. "a 40-second render" beats "a significant latency".
- A reaction before an explanation: "That's the part that surprises me." then the reason.
- Numbers rounded the way people say them: "about forty thousand", not "41,237".
- Repeat a key term on purpose. Repetition is how listeners hold a thread; it reads badly on paper and fine in the ear.

## What kills it

- Two hosts who agree with each other for five minutes. Manufacture friction from real tradeoffs, not fake conflict.
- Summarising the article instead of arguing about it. If the episode is only the source re-read, why would anyone listen?
- "As we discussed earlier" — the listener was there, they know.
- Invented anecdotes, invented statistics, invented quotes. Cut them. A gap is survivable; a fabricated number is not.
- Enthusiasm markers: "amazing", "incredible", "super interesting". Replace with the specific thing that is interesting.
- Reading a list. Convert to prose or drop it.
- Sound effects and music cues you were not asked for.

## Structure for a 5-minute episode

| Turns | Job |
| --- | --- |
| 1–2 | The hook: the surprising fact or the question the episode answers. No throat-clearing, no "welcome to the show". |
| 3–8 | The setup: why this matters, what the naive assumption is. |
| 9–20 | The substance: two or three points, each with a concrete example or number. |
| 21–26 | The objection: what does not work, what is oversold, what the source got wrong. |
| 27–32 | The takeaway: one sentence a listener could repeat to a colleague. |
| last | Stop. No outro music, no "thanks for listening" unless the user asked for a hosted format. |

Scale by adding substance turns in the middle, not by stretching the intro or the outro.

## Audio tags

Emotional tags are allowed and help, but only from the documented set. Put them at the start of a sentence or turn: `[curious] So what changed?`

Use at most one tag per two or three turns. A tag on every turn sounds manic. Plain text is the default.

## Language

Write in the source's language. If the source mixes languages, pick the one the user asked for and keep it consistent — a mid-turn switch will be pronounced with the wrong phonetics.

Keep proper nouns in their original form. Do not transliterate unless the source does.

## Final pass, before rendering

Read the script as if aloud. Anywhere you stumble, the voice will too. Anywhere you would skip, the listener will.
